## Introduction

[Alberta UFO reports]({{ 'report-data/' | relative_url }}) often rise for reasons that are more ordinary than the word “UFO” suggests. The province has wide horizons, dark rural roads, busy urban air corridors, aurora watchers, doorbell cameras, dashcams and many people who know the sky well enough to notice when something seems wrong. But many sighting waves are driven by a smaller set of repeat causes: bright meteors, satellite trains, re-entering space debris, aircraft lights, planets, weather effects and periods when public attention pushes more people to look up. That does not make every report worthless. It means [Alberta]({{ 'what-makes-albertas-ufo-record-so/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history has to be read as a mixture of real sky events, human attention, uneven reporting and a small residue of cases that remain unclear because the evidence is incomplete rather than because they prove something extraordinary.

The key point is that a “wave” is not automatically a wave of unknown craft. It may be a wave of witnesses seeing the same fireball, the same Starlink pass, the same aircraft pattern, or the same bright planet under unusually favourable viewing conditions. The most useful question is not “Was it a UFO?” but “What changed in the sky, in the witnesses, or in the reporting system at that moment?”

## Why Alberta produces sudden clusters

Alberta is a good place for dramatic sky reports because the province combines large open views with urban populations that can generate many simultaneous witnesses. A bright object over the Prairies can be seen from Calgary, Edmonton, rural central Alberta and sometimes neighbouring provinces within seconds. That makes one event feel like many incidents when it appears on social media, in emergency calls and in UFO databases.

This is especially clear with meteors. On 22 February 2021, a morning fireball over Alberta generated more than 500 reports to the American Meteor Society, with the event mainly seen from Alberta but also from British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Montana. That case shows why Alberta sighting spikes can be misleading if counted too simply. Hundreds of reports may describe one natural object, not hundreds of separate anomalies. The same pattern applies when an event is caught on doorbell cameras, vehicle cameras and phones: better documentation can strengthen identification, but it can also inflate public excitement before the explanation catches up.

UFO survey data points in the same direction. The 2024 Canadian UFO Survey reported that Alberta accounted for about 12 per cent of Canadian reports, broadly in line with its share of national population, while Calgary and Edmonton were among the metropolitan areas with the most reports that year. It also found that most reported UFOs were simple lights in the sky, with nocturnal lights making up 45.2 per cent of cases and close encounters less than 1.5 per cent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canadianuforeport.com/survey/essay/2024Surveyessay.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.com">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO ReportMicrosoft Word - 2024Surveyessay...</span></span></span> In other words, Alberta’s reported activity is significant, but much of it sits in the least diagnostic category: lights seen at night with limited distance, altitude and speed information.

## Meteors, satellites and sudden call spikes

The most common Alberta “UFO wave” mechanism is a real, visible event that many people interpret before they have enough context. A meteor is brief, bright and fast; a satellite train is orderly and strange-looking; space debris can fragment into several glowing pieces; aircraft lights can appear to hover or merge depending on direction and distance. Each can produce sincere reports from careful witnesses.

Meteors are the easiest to misunderstand because they arrive without warning. A fireball can be brighter than Venus, split apart, leave a trail and appear lower than it really is. In 2018, Global News described reports, photos and video “flooding in” after a strange bright light was seen across northern Alberta; the story concerned scientists searching for possible meteorites after a fireball report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://globalnews.ca/news/3973373/fireball-alberta-meteorites-meteor/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalnews.ca">[Global News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalnews.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global NewsScientists hunting for meteorites after fireball reported in...January 18, 2018 — 18 Jan 2018 — Reports, photos and video cam...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 18, 2018</span></span></span> In 2020, another apparent meteor was captured by a Sunshine Village camera over the Rocky Mountains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://globalnews.ca/news/6571051/apparent-meteor-calgary-banff/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalnews.ca">[Global News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalnews.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">apparent meteor calgary banff</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global NewsApparent meteor seen streaking across the night sky in...February 19, 2020 — 19 Feb 2020 — One of the cameras at Sunshine Vil...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 19, 2020</span></span></span> These are not weak sightings. Re-entry events are a related but more dramatic source of confusion. They can move more slowly than meteors, break into multiple glowing fragments and last long enough for many people to film them. This matters for Alberta because a re-entering object over Calgary or the Prairies may look more structured than a single meteor. The visual impression can be “formation”, “craft breaking apart” or “multiple objects”, even when the cause is a satellite or rocket body burning up.

The Canadian UFO Survey’s own duration guidance is useful here: short-duration events are usually [fireballs]({{ 'fireballs/' | relative_url }}) or bolides, while very long-duration sightings are often astronomical objects moving slowly with Earth’s rotation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canadianuforeport.com/survey/essay/2024Surveyessay.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.com">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO ReportMicrosoft Word - 2024Surveyessay...</span></span></span> That does not identify every Alberta report by itself, but it gives readers a practical first filter. Seconds suggest meteor or re-entry. A few minutes suggest satellites, aircraft, drones or drifting objects. An hour or more often points to planets, stars, atmospheric effects or a misjudged stationary light.

## Pandemic skywatching and public attention

The pandemic period matters because it changed both behaviour and attention. People were outside differently, commuting less, spending more time at home, watching the sky from gardens and streets, and sharing short videos rapidly. Alberta’s UFO record during this period is therefore not just a sky story; it is also a reporting story.

The 2021 Global News report on Alberta RCMP dispatchers linked increased skywatching during COVID-19 with growing interest in astronomy and unusual calls to 911. It quoted a Red Deer operations manager describing themes around UFO sightings and satellites, especially SpaceX-related calls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://globalnews.ca/news/7827693/alberta-rcmp-ufo-sightings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalnews.ca">[Global News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalnews.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">alberta rcmp ufo sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global News&#x27;Aliens are coming&#x27;: Alberta RCMP 911 dispatchers fielding...May 2, 2021 — 2 May 2021 — &#x27;Aliens are coming&#x27;: Alberta RCMP 911...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2, 2021</span></span></span> This is an important detail because the reports were not all routed through UFO groups. They entered emergency-call systems, local media and social media, each of which can amplify a cluster.

The 2024 Canadian UFO Survey also treated the pandemic period cautiously. It stated that a general increase in reports in 2020 was “undoubtedly related” to the onset of COVID-19 in some way, while later changes may have reflected suppression effects, media interest and social media rather than a simple dramatic rise in sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canadianuforeport.com/survey/essay/2024Surveyessay.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.com">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO ReportMicrosoft Word - 2024Surveyessay...</span></span></span> That wording is deliberately careful: the data show variation, but the causes include attention, reporting pathways and collection methods.

This is especially relevant in Alberta because social media groups devoted to aurora, weather and local skywatching can turn one bright object into a province-wide conversation within minutes. That can be helpful when multiple videos allow triangulation or expert identification. It can also be misleading when early captions frame an event as alien, military or mysterious before astronomers, meteor networks or satellite trackers have time to check it.

## Why “more reports” is not the same as “better evidence”

A rise in Alberta reports can mean several different things. It can mean more unusual objects were seen. It can mean more ordinary objects were noticed. It can mean more people had cameras. It can mean the same event was reported to several databases. It can also mean a new reporting app or social media channel changed the count.

Canada’s own reporting system makes this hard to untangle. The Sky Canada Project, led by the Office of the Chief Science Advisor of Canada, found that Canadian UAP reports are scattered across multiple government and non-government organisations, with no cohesive and standardised system for reporting and follow-up. It estimated that public sources put Canadian UAP reports at roughly 600 to 1,000 annually, while also noting that many Canadians would not know whom to contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[Science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span></span></span>

That fragmentation affects Alberta directly. A witness might call RCMP, contact an astronomy group, file with a civilian UFO database, post in a local Facebook group, send video to a news outlet, or do nothing. A pilot report might enter Transport Canada’s Civil Aviation Daily Occurrence Reporting System, while a casual witness report might never be investigated. Sky Canada noted that Transport Canada and NAV CANADA have mechanisms for aviation-related reports, but most organisations do not investigate sightings unless they touch national security, transportation safety or public safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[Science.gc.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada</span></span></span>

## How to separate patterns from proof

The best way to interpret Alberta sighting waves is to treat them as clues, not conclusions. A cluster can reveal something real about the sky, but the pattern has to survive basic checks before it becomes evidence of anything unusual.

A practical Alberta-focused filter looks like this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Time and duration:** A flash lasting seconds points first to meteor or re-entry. A line of lights lasting minutes points to satellites. A bright “stationary” light lasting much longer may be a planet, star, aircraft approaching head-on or distant tower light.
* **Direction and movement:** Objects moving steadily across the sky often match satellites or aircraft. Objects near the western or eastern horizon after sunset or before sunrise may be sunlight reflecting from satellites still above Earth’s shadow.
* **Number of witnesses:** Many witnesses can strengthen a case, but only if their locations, times and directions are recorded. Many people seeing the same fireball does not make it more anomalous; it makes the natural event easier to reconstruct.
* **Camera evidence:** Video helps most when it includes the horizon, stars, landmarks, time, location and unedited duration. A zoomed-in light against a black sky is often less useful than a wider, less dramatic clip.
* **Reporting pathway:** A CADORS pilot report, an RCMP call, a meteor-network entry and a social media post do not carry the same evidential weight. Each has different standards, purposes and limitations.

</div>

This approach does not dismiss witnesses. It protects them from bad explanations. Many Albertans who report meteors, satellite trains or re-entry events are observing genuine phenomena that are beautiful, rare-looking and worth documenting. The problem comes when a pattern is treated as proof before basic sky checks are made.

The 2024 survey’s reliability findings reinforce the point. It reported that the average reliability rating of Canadian UFO reports was just under 5, meaning many cases involved only witness forms without extensive supporting documentation or investigation; well-investigated cases were only a small fraction of the data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canadianuforeport.com/survey/essay/2024Surveyessay.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canadianuforeport.com">[Canadian UFO Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canadianuforeport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Canadian UFO Report Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canadian UFO ReportMicrosoft Word - 2024Surveyessay...</span></span></span> For Alberta, that means the strongest cases are not necessarily the most viral ones. They are the ones with precise timing, multiple independent witnesses, instrument data, flight or satellite checks, and a clear chain of documentation.

## What Alberta waves really tell us

Alberta’s sighting waves matter because they show how modern UFO history is made. A light appears; people film it; dispatchers, journalists, astronomers, pilots or hobbyists respond; the event is labelled, relabelled and archived. Sometimes the answer is quick, as with satellite trains. Sometimes it requires trajectory work, as with a fireball. Sometimes the record remains unresolved because the original report did not contain enough information to test.

The most balanced reading is that Alberta has plenty of real sky surprises, but most rises in reports are not evidence of a hidden pattern of extraordinary craft. They are usually the result of ordinary mechanisms becoming visible to many people at once, especially during periods of heightened attention. Meteors, Starlink passes, re-entering space debris, aircraft and bright planets explain a large share of what people mean when they say “UFO” in everyday speech.

The remaining cases should not be inflated or ignored. They belong in a narrower category: reports that are unresolved because the data are incomplete, the witness description is unusual, or the available checks do not settle the matter. Alberta’s UFO history is strongest when it keeps those categories separate. A debunked satellite train, a confirmed fireball and a genuinely puzzling report can all be part of the same provincial record, but they do not carry the same meaning.
