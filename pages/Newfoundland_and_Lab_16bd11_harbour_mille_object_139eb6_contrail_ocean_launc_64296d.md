## Introduction

One of the most discussed ordinary explanations for the [Harbour Mille]({{ 'harbour-mille/' | relative_url }}) sighting is not a missile, a secret launch, or an object emerging from the Atlantic at all. Instead, it is the possibility that witnesses were looking at a distant high-altitude aircraft whose sunlit contrail created a powerful illusion. Under the right winter conditions, a jet flying towards an observer can appear to be climbing vertically from the horizon, while its illuminated condensation trail can resemble smoke, fire, or rocket exhaust. The theory does not prove what Harbour Mille residents saw on 25 January 2010, but it offers a plausible mechanism that matches several key features of the reports and photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrail" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

Within the wider Harbour Mille debate, the contrail explanation matters because it addresses the most striking claim: that a large object seemed to rise from the sea. Rather than requiring an actual launch from the ocean, it suggests that distance, perspective and low-angle sunlight may have combined to create a misleading visual impression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

## Could a contrail look like a sea launch?

The central idea behind the contrail theory is surprisingly simple. If an aircraft is travelling roughly towards an observer rather than across their field of view, its motion becomes difficult to judge. Instead of seeing a plane moving left-to-right, observers mainly see it growing slightly larger as it approaches. Because there are few visual clues to its true distance, the aircraft can appear almost stationary while its contrail seems to rise from the horizon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrail" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For someone standing on a Newfoundland coastline looking out across open water, the effect can be especially dramatic. The horizon provides a strong visual reference line. A distant aircraft approaching from beyond that horizon may appear to emerge directly from the ocean. If the aircraft itself is difficult to see but the contrail is brightly illuminated, the eye naturally focuses on the trail rather than the aircraft. The result can resemble a vertical plume extending upward from the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

This is not merely a theoretical possibility. Similar “mystery missile” incidents have occurred elsewhere. In late 2010, a widely publicised plume off the California coast was initially reported as a possible missile launch before aviation specialists, military officials and flight-track analysis concluded that a distant aircraft contrail seen from an unusual angle was the most likely explanation. Experts noted that an aircraft flying towards the observer could appear to be climbing vertically when it was actually flying level at cruising altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

The comparison does not prove that Harbour Mille involved the same phenomenon. It does, however, demonstrate that experienced observers, journalists and even officials can initially mistake a contrail for a launch plume when perspective is deceptive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

## Why sunset light can mimic flame or smoke

The timing of the Harbour Mille sighting is one reason sceptical investigators found the contrail explanation attractive. Witnesses reported the event during the late afternoon or early evening, close to sunset. That is exactly when high-altitude contrails can become unusually bright and dramatic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://macleans.ca/general/alleged-missiles-spotted-over-newfoundland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: macleans.ca">[Macleans.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">macleans.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alleged missiles spotted over Newfoundland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 28, 2010 — 28 Jan 2010 — Three long, thin glimmering &quot;bullet-like&quot; objects with tails of fire and smoke were sighted in the sky o...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2010</span></span></span>

At cruising altitude, an aircraft may still be illuminated by the Sun even after the landscape below has entered shadow. Ice crystals within the contrail reflect and scatter sunlight, often producing vivid white, orange, gold or reddish colours. To an observer on the ground, the bright leading point of a distant aircraft can resemble an engine flame, while the illuminated trail behind it can look like smoke from a rocket or missile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/news/feature-articles/trail-contrails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: earthdata.nasa.gov">[NASA Earthdata+2Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">earthdata.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">trail contrails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA EarthdataOn the Trail of Contrailsby L Naranjo · 2020 — Contrails are the linear clouds etched across the skies by high-altitude air...</span></span></span>

Several features reported in Harbour Mille fit this general pattern:

* Witnesses described elongated, missile-like shapes rather than clearly identifiable aircraft.
* Some accounts mentioned apparent flames or glowing exhaust.
* The objects were observed against a low-sun winter sky.
* Photographs captured a bright trail but did not provide reliable information about distance, altitude or scale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://macleans.ca/general/alleged-missiles-spotted-over-newfoundland/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: macleans.ca">[Macleans.ca+2UPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">macleans.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alleged missiles spotted over Newfoundland</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 28, 2010 — 28 Jan 2010 — Three long, thin glimmering &quot;bullet-like&quot; objects with tails of fire and smoke were sighted in the sky o...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2010</span></span></span>

None of these observations uniquely indicate a contrail. However, they are consistent with the way a distant jet and its condensation trail can appear under twilight lighting conditions. Aviation and atmospheric specialists have long noted that sunset illumination can make ordinary contrails appear unusually dense, bright and rocket-like.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.armscontrolwonk.com/archive/203136/it-aint-no-thing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: armscontrolwonk.com">[armscontrolwonk.com+2Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">armscontrolwonk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">it aint no thing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">it aint no thing</span></span></span>

## Why the illusion can seem convincing

One reason the Harbour Mille reports attracted attention is that some witnesses were convinced they saw an object emerge from the water rather than merely appear near the horizon. Human perception, however, is not very good at estimating distance over open ocean.

When looking across a bay or towards the horizon, there are few nearby landmarks that help the brain judge how far away an object really is. A jet at 30,000 feet can be many tens or even hundreds of kilometres distant while still appearing close to the horizon. Because the observer cannot easily perceive its forward motion, the object may seem to rise upward from a fixed point on the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

The shape of the contrail can strengthen the illusion. Winds at high altitude often spread the older portions of a contrail while leaving the newest section narrow and bright. That can create a tapered plume resembling rocket exhaust. In photographs lacking scale references, the effect may appear even more dramatic than it did in person.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aviation.stackexchange.com/questions/77079/is-this-contrail-from-an-airplane-or-a-rocket" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aviation.stackexchange.com">[Aviation Stack Exchange+2Contrail Science Overflow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aviation.stackexchange.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">is this contrail from an airplane or a rocket</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The aircraft is flying straight and level and the contrail behind it is being distorted by the winds aloft. Importantly, an observer can honestly and accurately describe what they perceived while still misinterpreting the object's true geometry. The contrail explanation does not require fabrication or poor observation. It only requires a viewing angle that disguises the aircraft's actual position and direction of travel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

## Where the contrail explanation remains unproved

Although the contrail theory is often presented as the leading conventional explanation, it is not a complete solution to every question raised by the Harbour Mille case.

The strongest limitation is the absence of a publicly demonstrated match between the sighting and a specific aircraft flight. In some well-known contrail misidentifications, investigators were eventually able to correlate photographs, radar information and flight [records]({{ 'records-45a181/' | relative_url }}). No similarly definitive public reconstruction has emerged for Harbour Mille.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

There are also witness details that supporters of the mystery interpretation continue to emphasise. Some observers reported multiple objects, unusual trajectories, or behaviour that did not obviously resemble a single aircraft. Because the public record is incomplete and the photographs are limited, those claims cannot be fully tested today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://reddeeradvocate.com/2010/01/30/woman-who-claims-she-saw-missiles-says-area-not-used-by-model-hobbyists/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reddeeradvocate.com">[Red Deer Advocate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reddeeradvocate.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">woman who claims she saw missiles says area not used by model hobbyists</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Red Deer AdvocateWoman who claims she saw missiles says area not used...30 Jan 2010 — Emmy Pardy said she clearly saw three huge “grey b...</span></span></span>

As a result, the contrail theory should be viewed as a plausible explanatory model rather than a proven identification. It successfully explains how a silent object could appear to launch from the ocean and why it might display a bright, missile-like plume under winter sunset conditions. What it does not yet provide is a publicly documented flight-path reconstruction that conclusively closes the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

## Why many investigators regard it as the strongest ordinary explanation

Among non-extraordinary explanations, the sunlit contrail theory remains influential because it accounts for several otherwise puzzling elements with a single mechanism: the apparent ocean launch, the vertical appearance, the glowing exhaust, the missile-like silhouette and the timing near sunset. Similar optical misinterpretations have been documented in other widely publicised cases, demonstrating that the phenomenon is real and repeatable rather than hypothetical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Christian Science Monitor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>

For the Harbour Mille incident specifically, the key lesson is that dramatic appearances over open water can be deceptive. A distant aircraft operating normally at high altitude can, under the right geometry and lighting, look remarkably like something far more exotic. That does not settle every aspect of the case, but it explains why many researchers regard the apparent “launch from the sea” as the weakest part of the mystery rather than its strongest evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_California_contrail_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Contrail Science Overflow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">2010 California contrail incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2010 California contrail incident</span></span></span>
